Short answer first: it's probably not that you're unusually loud. It's that the wall between you and your neighbor is doing less work than the wall you grew up with. If you moved to Japan from North America, the UK, or much of continental Europe, your reference point for "normal indoor volume" was almost certainly set by brick, block, or poured-concrete construction with real mass in the party wall. A lot of Japanese housing — especially the wood-frame (*mokuzō*) apartments that make up the majority of the rental market — doesn't have that mass. The complaint isn't a judgment on your behavior. It's a mismatch between your habits and the building.

## Why should I care if I'm not planning to move?

Three groups end up here:

- <strong>New arrivals</strong> who got a complaint slip, a knock from the landlord, or a passive-aggressive note and want to understand whether they did something wrong or just live somewhere different.
- <strong>People apartment-hunting in Japan</strong> who want to know what "thin walls" actually means in practice before they sign a lease.
- <strong>Long-term residents</strong> who've absorbed the local norms and are curious why this keeps coming up for newer arrivals — useful context if you're advising a friend or colleague.

## The wall difference, in physical terms

Sound insulation is mostly about mass. A heavier, denser wall absorbs more sound energy before it can pass through — this is the "mass law" of acoustics, and it's not specific to Japan. What's specific to Japan is which wall type is common.

| | Typical in much of North America/Europe | Typical in Japanese rental housing |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <strong>Common structure</strong> | Brick, concrete block, or poured concrete with real mass in exterior and sometimes party walls | Wood-frame (*mokuzō*) with thin gypsum board on light studs is the largest share of the rental market |
| <strong>What stops sound</strong> | Wall mass itself does most of the work | Depends heavily on the specific building — some newer reinforced-concrete (RC) apartments are comparable to what you're used to; older wood-frame buildings are not |
| <strong>What travels easily</strong> | Airborne sound (voices, music) is the main concern | Both airborne sound <strong>and</strong> impact sound (footsteps, dropped objects) travel more easily through lighter floor/wall assemblies |

This is why the same volume of TV, conversation, or a video call that was inaudible in your last apartment might be clearly audible to a neighbor here — the wall simply isn't removing as much energy from the sound before it reaches the other side.

## "But my building looks modern"

Appearance doesn't tell you the structure. A building can look new and still be wood-frame, and a building can look plain and be solid reinforced concrete (RC) or steel-reinforced concrete (SRC), which performs much closer to what you're used to. If you're not sure which you're in, ask the property agent for the structure type (*kōzō*) — RC and SRC buildings are generally the quieter end of the Japanese rental market; wood-frame (*mokuzō*) and light-gauge steel (*keiryō tekkotsu*) are the end where you're most likely to hear, and be heard by, your neighbors.

## What Japanese law actually says about it

Unlike some countries, Japan doesn't set a mandatory minimum sound-insulation rating for most housing. What governs neighbor noise disputes here is a completely different legal concept: *juninin gendo* (受忍限度), the "limit of tolerance" — a case-by-case standard weighing noise level, time of day, frequency, and neighborhood character, rather than a fixed decibel line written into the building code. This matters practically: there's no official certificate that tells you "this apartment is quiet enough." The building's structure type is the closest thing you'll get to that signal before you move in.

## The unwritten part: time-of-day norms

Even in a well-insulated building, Japan has stronger informal norms around what's acceptable after a certain hour than many Western countries do. Vacuuming, washing machines, phone calls on speaker, and video calls with the volume up are treated as fine during the day but are commonly avoided after around 9–10pm, well before what would be considered "late" in many other countries' apartment culture. This isn't written into any contract — it's absorbed by watching what everyone else does, which is exactly the kind of thing a new arrival has no way to know in advance.

## What to actually change

- <strong>Lower your default baseline</strong>, not just your peak volume. A phone call at a volume that felt private in a concrete building can carry through a wood-frame wall.
- <strong>Move calls and video chats away from shared walls</strong> if you can — a few feet of extra air and furniture in between makes a real difference in a light-frame building.
- <strong>Treat footsteps and dropped objects as seriously as voice</strong>. Impact sound (walking, especially in hard-soled slippers, or letting things drop) is often the bigger complaint driver in Japanese apartments, not conversation.
- <strong>Shift anything loud earlier in the evening</strong>. The informal 9–10pm cutoff is worth respecting even if your lease says nothing about it.
- <strong>If you got a complaint, don't take it as an accusation of rudeness</strong>. In most cases it means the building is doing less acoustic work than you're used to, and a small habit change resolves it without any renovation.

## The takeaway

You're not imagining that people seem more sensitive to noise here, and you're probably not imagining that you're being louder than you think. Both are true at once: Japanese wood-frame construction removes less sound energy than the walls you may be used to, and local norms around evening quiet are stricter than in many other countries. Neither is a flaw in your behavior — it's a different baseline, and once you know what it is, it's a short adjustment rather than a mystery.
