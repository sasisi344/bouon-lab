Short answer: Japanese streamers and VTubers don't usually soundproof the room they stream in — they build or buy a small enclosed booth inside it, sized in tatami-mat units (0.8 to 1.5 <em>jō</em>, roughly 1.3–2.5m²), and stream from inside that box. This looks like overkill to a Western creator used to hanging acoustic foam on bedroom walls, but it's the direct result of how Japanese housing actually works, not a stylistic preference.

## Why "soundproof the room" isn't the default approach here

Two structural facts about Japanese housing push the solution toward a booth-in-a-room rather than a treated room:

- <strong>Most streamers rent, and Japanese leases prohibit wall modification.</strong> You can't legally staple foam to a rental wall or add mass-loaded vinyl behind drywall the way a homeowner elsewhere might. Anything permanent is off the table by default.
- <strong>Walls are genuinely thinner than what many international streamers are used to.</strong> Wood-frame apartment construction (the dominant type) transmits mid-to-high frequency sound — voice, keyboard clatter, laughter — more readily than concrete or brick construction common elsewhere. Treating the room's walls wouldn't meaningfully fix the two directions that matter most: your voice bleeding out to neighbors, and your neighbors' noise bleeding into your mic.

So the market solved it differently: instead of treating the room, build a self-contained box that doesn't touch the walls at all.

## The three tiers streamers actually use

| Tier | Typical cost | What it solves |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| <strong>DIY closet booth</strong> | ¥30,000〜 (~$200) | Converts an existing closet into a vocal/recording booth using foam-faced panels tensioned in place with rods — zero wall contact, fully reversible for a lease |
| <strong>Rental prefab booth</strong> | ¥13,000–35,000/month + ¥100,000〜 setup | A factory-built booth delivered and installed, returnable when you move or stop streaming |
| <strong>Purchased prefab unit</strong> | ¥600,000–1,300,000 for the most common 1.2–1.5 <em>jō</em> size | The long-term option for creators streaming as an ongoing income source, not a hobby experiment |

The DIY tier is disproportionately popular among individual VTubers and new streamers specifically because it's the only one that requires zero landlord negotiation and zero long-term commitment — you're testing whether streaming is something you'll stick with before spending real money.

## What livestreaming actually needs that "soundproofing" alone doesn't fix

A generic "soundproof room" search misses half the problem for a streamer. Two issues matter more for live audio than for, say, practicing an instrument:

- <strong>Room echo, not just outside noise.</strong> A completely empty room with hard walls creates reverb that makes a voice sound distant and hollow to viewers, even if no outside noise leaks in at all. This is why the small-booth approach works so well for streaming specifically: a tight, foam-lined box kills reverb almost automatically just by being small and absorptive, which is exactly what a livestream mic needs.
- <strong>PC and fan noise picked up by the mic.</strong> High-end streaming PCs generate real heat and real fan noise inside a sealed booth. Japanese setups commonly solve this by running the PC tower itself outside the booth (through a cable pass-through) and keeping only the monitor, keyboard, and mic inside — a detail that's easy to miss if you're planning a build based on Western streaming-room photos, which usually show the whole rig in one open room.

## The size that actually works for streaming (and why smaller fails)

Japanese creators consistently report that starting too small is the single most expensive mistake. An 0.8-<em>jō</em> booth fits a person and a mic but not a proper desk setup with multiple monitors — many streamers who start there end up replacing it with a 1.5-<em>jō</em> unit within a year, effectively paying for two booths instead of one. 1.5 <em>jō</em> (roughly a 120cm-wide desk plus chair, camera distance, and lighting) is the size most repeat buyers land on and rarely need to upsize from.

## If you're setting this up outside Japan

The booth-first approach isn't uniquely Japanese in principle — it works anywhere with the same constraints: renting, thin walls, or simply not wanting to commit to permanent construction. What's specific to Japan is the maturity of the market around it: standardized prefab sizes, an active resale market for used units (so the purchase tier isn't a sunk cost), and a rental tier that doesn't exist in most other countries' streaming-gear markets. A streamer outside Japan can replicate the booth logic with a DIY panel build even without access to the Japanese prefab brands.
